In Sweden, women are 66 percent of the students who finish high school in the top 10 percent of their cohort. By any measure of academic preparation, the pipeline into selective university programs is not a place where Swedish women are losing ground. They are the clear majority of the people standing at its entrance.

Among those same top-decile students, women are 26 percentage points less likely than men to put a STEM program first on their university application.

That combination is the puzzle. It is also, it turns out, not a Swedish peculiarity.

## Two gaps that get added together and shouldn't be

Women make up about 35 percent of STEM graduates worldwide, and that share has not moved in a decade. The number is usually explained one of two ways: girls are not being prepared for or steered toward the quantitative track, or women who *are* prepared choose something else. Call the first the **pipeline gap** and the second the **choice gap**. They imply very different policies — one points at schools and curricula, the other at what happens at the moment of application — and almost every dataset in the world blends them together, because what we normally observe is enrollment, which is preparation and choice and admission all at once.

Separating them takes an unusual kind of data: you have to observe what a student was eligible for *and* what they asked for, for the same person, at scale, in more than one country.

Coordinated admissions systems produce exactly that. In a growing share of the world — [about 60 percent of countries now use some coordinated mechanism](/projects/ccas-review) — students submit a ranked list of college-and-major combinations to a central platform, eligibility is a published function of exam scores or grades, and a deferred-acceptance algorithm assigns each student to the highest-ranked option they qualify for. Two things follow. Students have little incentive to misreport their preferences, so the list is informative about what they actually want. And admission runs on a score computed identically for women and men, so we can compare women and men with the same academic standing facing the same rules.

We assembled application microdata from ten such systems on five continents: Australia, Brazil, Chile, China, Finland, Greece, Spain, Sweden, Taiwan and Uganda. Then we looked at high achievers — students in the top 10 percent of each country's own performance distribution — for whom access constraints are least likely to bind, and asked how often each group ranks a STEM program first.

## The pipeline gap varies enormously. The choice gap doesn't.

<figure>

  ![Two panels of horizontal bars for ten countries. Left: the pipeline gap, women minus men as a share of top-10% students, runs from -23 points in Uganda and -21 in Taiwan to +26 in Spain and +32 in Sweden, with bars on both sides of zero. Right: the choice gap, the female-minus-male difference in ranking a STEM program first among those same top-10% students, is negative in all ten countries, from -11 in Uganda to -36 in China.](./images/pipeline-vs-choice.png)

  <figcaption>Preparation swings both ways across countries. What high achievers apply to does not: every bar on the right points the same direction.</figcaption>
</figure>

The left panel is what you would expect from a story about development and gender norms. Women are underrepresented among top scorers in Taiwan, Chile and Uganda, and overrepresented in Australia, China, Greece, Spain and Sweden. The spread is 55 percentage points wide, from Uganda at 23 points below parity to Sweden at 32 above.

The right panel does not vary in the same way. It does not vary in sign at all. In every one of the ten systems, high-achieving women are less likely than high-achieving men to rank a STEM program first — by about 24 percentage points on average across settings, and by more than 20 points in eight of the ten. It holds when we count STEM anywhere on the application list rather than only first. It holds at every threshold we tried: pooling the ten countries, the gap is 21.5, 22.4, 22.4 and 21.9 points at the top 5, 10, 20 and 30 percent, a range of less than one point while the group being examined gets six times bigger.

And critically, the two panels do not line up. Sweden has the largest pipeline advantage for women in our data and the third-largest choice gap. Uganda has the largest pipeline disadvantage and the smallest choice gap.

## The paradox is in one margin, not the other

That inverse relationship is not a coincidence of two countries. Plot both gaps against a measure of how gender-equal each country's labor market is — one minus the gender wage gap — and the two margins go in opposite directions.

<figure>

  ![Two scatter plots of ten countries against a wage parity index running from about 0.5 to 1.0. Top panel: the pipeline gap rises steeply with wage parity, from about -22 points in Uganda at the low end to above +20 in Sweden, Spain and Greece at the high end, crossing zero around 0.8. Bottom panel: the STEM first-choice gap sits between -11 and -36 across the whole range, with a flat, slightly downward fitted line and no country above -11.](./images/wage-parity.png)

  <figcaption>As wage parity rises, women's representation among top students rises with it. Their propensity to rank STEM first does not.</figcaption>
</figure>

This is a version of what the literature calls the gender-equality paradox, and it has been argued over for years. What the decomposition adds is that the paradox lives in one component and not the other. Aggregate measures of women's STEM participation combine the two margins, which respond to gender equality with opposite signs — so the aggregate can look flat while one of its parts is moving systematically. Ten countries is not many, the slopes are imprecise, and wage parity travels with a great deal else. We report the pattern as description, not mechanism.

## It isn't that women avoid selective, high-paying fields

The obvious competing explanation is that this has nothing to do with STEM specifically: high-achieving women might simply be less drawn to competitive, high-return programs, or be pulled toward other elite fields.

The same data can test that, because within a country the pipeline component is identical across fields — so any difference between fields is attributable to choice alone. Health goes the other way. Conditional on being high-achieving, women are *more* likely than men to rank a health program first, in eight of ten countries on the first-choice measure and in all ten when STEM can appear anywhere on the list. Business and law shows no systematic pattern at all: positive in four of the nine countries that report it, negative in the rest.

The sharpest way to put it is as a counterfactual. Suppose you closed the pipeline gap completely — made the top decile exactly half women — and changed nothing about application behavior. High-achieving STEM applicants would still be between 57 and 75 percent male, in every country we observe. Run the same exercise for health and you get between 34 and 52 percent male. The two ranges do not overlap.

## The gap opens at application, not at admission

Because these systems record both what students rank and where they are placed, we can follow high achievers through the whole funnel.

<figure>

  ![A chart with four markers per country showing women's share at each successive stage: in the top 10%, ranked STEM anywhere, ranked STEM first, and assigned to STEM. In every country the share falls sharply between being a top student and ranking STEM, then stays roughly flat through assignment.](./images/stem-funnel.png)

  <figcaption>Pooled across the ten systems, women are 51.2% of the top decile, 38.6% of those ranking STEM anywhere, 34.0% of those ranking it first, and 34.7% of those assigned to a STEM program.</figcaption>
</figure>

Almost all of the shortfall happens between the second and third columns of that sequence — that is, in the application itself. The assignment stage adds essentially nothing, and what it adds carries no systematic sign. The larger of the two application margins is not where STEM sits on the list, but whether it appears on the list at all.

This is a statement about the composition of cohorts, not about individual admission decisions, and we observe the program a student is assigned to rather than the one they eventually enroll in. But the location of the gap is clear enough. By the time the algorithm runs, the gap is already there.

## What we can't say, and why it still matters

Admissions records show what students ranked. They do not show the beliefs, expectations and information that produced the ranking. A student can report her preferences perfectly truthfully and still be reporting preferences formed under bad information about what a STEM degree involves, what it pays, or whether she would belong there. Our design closes off the most mechanical explanations — misreporting, list-length limits, differential admission standards — and it cannot reach that one.

Nor is stability across countries evidence that anything here is innate. Application choices are formed inside social, cultural and institutional environments, and the fact that ten very different environments produce gaps of similar sign tells you the drivers are at least partly shared. It does not tell you what they are.

What it does establish is where to look. If the gap opened at admission, the fix would be an admissions fix. If it opened in preparation, the fix would be curricular, and Sweden would be much closer to parity than it is. It opens in the weeks when a student sits down with a list of programs and decides what to put at the top — which happens to be a moment that coordinated platforms already touch, already instrument, and already use to deliver information at national scale. That is the part of the system [we build in](/projects/women-in-stem).
